# Intensive care and recovery after coronary bypass surgery

Elysian Biomedical LLP · Cardiac Surgery Education Series · Episode 03

English narration for ElevenLabs Alexander. Say ICU, IV, and ECG as separate letters. Record the paragraphs only. Keep the emergency instruction deliberate and audible.

## P01 · Waking up after surgery

You open your eyes after bypass surgery. There may be a breathing tube, a monitor beside you, and a nurse asking you to nod. What is each piece of equipment doing, and how does the team decide when you are ready for the next stage? Let's follow one patient from intensive care to the ward and then home. Your own recovery may look different.

## P02 · Breathing support changes as you wake

A ventilator may still be helping you breathe through a tube in your windpipe. The tube usually passes through the mouth. While it is there, you will be unable to speak. A nurse can ask questions you can answer with a nod or a head shake. Staff check how awake you are and how well you are breathing before they decide whether the tube can come out. After it is removed, some people still need extra oxygen through a mask or soft tubes at the nose. Ask the nurse what breathing support you are receiving.

## P03 · What the lines and monitors do

Different pieces of equipment tell the team different things. The heart monitor shows the rate and rhythm. A small sensor on a finger helps staff check how much oxygen your blood is carrying. A line in an artery can measure blood pressure continuously. IV lines can give fluids or medicines. A chest drain carries fluid from around the chest to a collection system. A bladder catheter may collect urine so staff can measure the amount. You may also have temporary pacing wires that can help support the heart rhythm if needed. The team decides when each support is still useful and when it can be removed.

## P04 · Comfort, alertness and a family member's role

Tell your nurse if pain makes it difficult to take a deeper breath, cough, or move comfortably. Staff can review your pain relief and show you how to do the breathing and movement exercises planned for you. Some people become unusually confused, restless or sleepy in intensive care. This can be delirium, a sudden change in attention and awareness that can come and go. If you notice a change in someone you know well, tell the nurse. Staff can assess what may be contributing. Ask whether familiar conversation, glasses or hearing aids would help.

## P05 · From intensive care to the ward

When the team decides you no longer need intensive care, you can move to a ward where staff continue to check your recovery. Your first activity might be sitting in a chair, standing with help, or taking a short walk with a nurse or physiotherapist. They choose what fits your condition and gradually build from there. Tell staff if you feel dizzy, unusually breathless or too uncomfortable to continue. Progress depends on how you are recovering, so the next step can differ from one person to another.

## P06 · Take home a plan you can use

Before leaving, ask the team to walk through your discharge plan with you. Which medicines are you taking now? What changed from before surgery? When should you contact someone about your dressings or wounds? If a vessel came from your arm or leg, you may have another wound that needs its own instructions. Ask how to build up activity and when it is safe to drive, lift, or go back to work. Arrange help at home if you need it. Keep your medicines list, follow-up details, and the number to call if you have a concern, including outside clinic hours.

## P07 · The first weeks and cardiac rehabilitation

At home you may tire more quickly, have less appetite, or find that your sleep and mood change. Let your team know if these problems persist or make it hard to manage. Build up activity using the plan you were given. Take your prescribed medicines and discuss any changes with the care team. Cardiac rehabilitation is a supervised programme that can include exercise, information about heart health, and support with confidence and stress. Ask how to join and what options are available where you live. Follow-up also helps the team review symptoms and keep your longer-term prevention plan on track.

## P08 · Who to call when something changes

Contact your care team promptly if a wound becomes increasingly painful or red, starts draining, or you develop a fever. Follow the urgent contact instructions you received if the team is unavailable. Call your local emergency number straight away for sudden chest pressure or discomfort that does not go away, severe trouble breathing, or possible signs of a stroke. Stroke signs can include a drooping face, sudden arm weakness, or difficulty speaking. Even one of these stroke signs needs emergency help straight away.

## P09 · Three questions to carry forward

At each step, ask what the team is watching, what you can safely do next, and whom to contact if something changes. Keep your own discharge instructions close by. In the next episode, we'll look at surgical replacement of the aortic valve.
