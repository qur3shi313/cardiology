# Surgical mitral valve repair

## P01 · A valve that leaks backward

The mitral valve sits between the left atrium and left ventricle. When the ventricle fills, the valve opens. When the ventricle squeezes, its two leaflets should meet so blood goes out toward the body. If one leaflet bends too far back, blood can leak into the left atrium. Today we’ll see a repair that keeps the person’s own valve.

## P02 · What the ultrasound shows

In this example, the leak comes from the back leaflet. Small, tough cords normally hold the leaflet edges in position as pressure rises. One of those cords has torn, so part of the leaflet moves too far into the atrium. An echocardiogram, or heart ultrasound, helps the team find the leaking part and judge the effect on the heart.

## P03 · When repair is an option

A leaking valve does not always need an operation right away. The team looks at how severe the leak is, any symptoms, and how the heart is responding. When this type of severe leak needs surgery and a durable repair is likely, repair is usually preferred. The surgeon tailors the repair to the exact part that is not working.

## P04 · Reaching the valve

The operation is done under general anaesthesia. In this example, a heart-lung machine supports circulation while the team works on the valve. The view now moves inside the left atrium. From here, the surgeon can examine the leaflet edges, their support cords and the rim around the valve. This is the same leaking section we saw before.

## P05 · Restoring the support

For this valve, the surgeon uses artificial cords to give the back leaflet support again. One end is attached to a muscle inside the left ventricle and the other to the leaflet edge. An annuloplasty band supports the rim around the valve. The band stays at the rim. The leaflets still open and close inside it.

## P06 · Does the valve close well?

When the heart resumes pumping, the team checks the repair with ultrasound. In our illustration, the two leaflets meet during the squeeze and the backward jet disappears. Between beats they still open to let the ventricle fill. The team looks for a remaining leak and makes sure the repair has not made the opening too narrow.

## P07 · Recovery and follow-up

After surgery the team monitors breathing and heart rhythm. Before discharge, the person gets advice on medicines, wound care, activity and when to return for follow-up. The team may recommend cardiac rehabilitation. Repair keeps the original valve, but it still needs checkups. Follow-up can detect a new leak or other changes before symptoms are obvious.

## P08 · Risks and warning signs

Bleeding, infection, rhythm changes and stroke are possible. A valve can leak again and need further treatment. Contact your team promptly for fever or a wound that is getting worse. Get emergency help for severe difficulty breathing or sudden signs of stroke, such as face droop or trouble speaking. Your discharge plan should say who to contact.

## P09 · Keeping the native valve

Here, artificial cords supported the back leaflet and a band supported the rim. The heart still uses its own leaflets. The team used ultrasound to check the change before recovery began.
