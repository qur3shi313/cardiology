# Surgical pulmonary valve replacement after tetralogy of Fallot repair

Elysian Biomedical LLP · Cardiac Surgery Education Series · Episode 08

English narration for ElevenLabs Alexander. Say tetralogy as teh-TRAL-uh-jee and Fallot as fa-LOH. Read MRI as separate letters. Record only the nine spoken paragraphs.

## P01 · A childhood repair and an adult follow-up

The pulmonary valve sits between the right ventricle and the artery that carries blood to the lungs. This story follows an adult who had surgery as a child for tetralogy of Fallot, a heart condition present at birth. The childhood operation helped blood get to the lungs. Years later, the team is checking how well the pulmonary valve closes.

## P02 · What happens when the valve leaks

When the right ventricle squeezes, blood should go out toward the lungs. Between beats, a healthy pulmonary valve closes. Here, some blood returns from the lung artery to the right ventricle. Over time, the extra volume can enlarge the ventricle. Some people notice less stamina or breathlessness. Others have changes on imaging before noticing symptoms.

## P03 · How the team chooses an approach

An ultrasound helps assess the valves and the heart’s pumping. A heart MRI can measure how much blood leaks back and show the size and function of the right ventricle. The congenital heart team compares the results with earlier tests, symptoms and the shape of the outflow. A catheter valve may suit some people. Here, the team recommends surgical replacement.

## P04 · Reaching the outflow

In this open operation, the patient is under general anaesthesia and a heart-lung machine supports blood flow. The surgeon reaches the area where blood leaves the right ventricle for the lungs. The previous repair matters, because the opening can have a different shape from a heart that has never been operated on. The team prepares this area to receive the new valve.

## P05 · Placing a new pulmonary valve

In this example, a tissue valve is secured in the prepared opening. Follow it into the path between the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery. The new valve stays there. Its leaflets can swing open toward the lungs when the ventricle squeezes, and they close when the ventricle relaxes. The aortic valve has not been changed.

## P06 · What the result looks like

After the repair site is closed and the heart takes over circulation again, the team checks the new valve and the right ventricle. In our illustration, blood still goes forward with each squeeze, and much less returns between beats. The team looks for residual leakage or narrowing. The new valve does not erase the history of congenital heart disease.

## P07 · Recovery and lifelong review

Early recovery includes monitoring the wound, breathing and heart rhythm. The care team provides a plan for medicines, movement and follow-up. Even after the wound heals, lifelong follow-up with congenital heart specialists remains important. They can track the valve, the right ventricle and heart rhythm over time. Tissue valves can wear, so another treatment may be needed later.

## P08 · Risks and when to get help

Bleeding, infection and rhythm problems are possible after surgery. The replacement can also develop problems over time. Follow the care team’s contact plan for fever, a wound that worsens or other changes they have told you about. Severe breathing difficulty or sudden stroke signs, such as face droop or trouble speaking, need emergency help.

## P09 · The valve stays in the right-heart pathway

This operation placed a new valve in the path from the right ventricle to the lungs. Our next episode, the Ross procedure, uses a different idea: the surgeon moves the person’s own pulmonary valve to the aortic position. We’ll show both locations and explain why both need attention afterward.
