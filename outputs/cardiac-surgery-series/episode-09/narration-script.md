# The Ross operation and the two valve positions

Elysian Biomedical LLP · Cardiac Surgery Education Series · Episode 09

English narration for ElevenLabs Alexander. Say Ross as one syllable. Record the paragraphs only.

## P01 · Two valves and one move

The Ross operation begins with a problem at the aortic valve, the valve that opens toward the body. In this operation, a surgeon uses the patient's own pulmonary valve to replace it. A donated human valve then takes over the pulmonary position. Follow the two valves and see why the team needs to check both of them afterward.

## P02 · What the damaged aortic valve does

A healthy aortic valve opens as the left ventricle pumps blood into the aorta. A narrowed valve can make it harder for blood to leave. A leaky valve can let some blood return to the ventricle after the heartbeat. The pulmonary valve sits on a separate route from the right ventricle to the lungs.

## P03 · Who might discuss it

Some adults who need aortic valve replacement may discuss the Ross operation with an experienced team. The team assesses both valves, the shape of the aortic root and the person's health and priorities. A damaged pulmonary valve or some inherited aortic conditions can make this approach unsuitable. Other choices include mechanical and tissue replacement valves.

## P04 · The patient's valve becomes the autograft

For this open operation, a heart-lung machine temporarily takes over circulation and oxygenation. The surgeon confirms that the pulmonary valve is suitable. In the example shown, the surgeon removes the pulmonary valve and its surrounding root as one unit for use in the aortic position. That piece of the patient's own tissue is called an autograft.

## P05 · The new aortic position

The damaged aortic valve is removed and the autograft takes its place. The surgeon reconnects the two coronary arteries to openings in the moved root so they can still receive blood from the aorta. The autograft now sits on the route from the left ventricle to the body, where pressures are higher.

## P06 · Rebuilding the outlet to the lungs

The right ventricle still needs a route to the lungs. A donated human valve and conduit, called a homograft, fills the place left by the moved pulmonary valve. In the completed repair, the patient's original pulmonary valve is working on the aortic side, and the donated valve works on the pulmonary side.

## P07 · What the team checks

As the heart resumes pumping, the team checks how both valves open and close and how blood travels through each outlet. This operation can avoid the need for lifelong anticoagulation solely because of a mechanical valve. A person may still need blood-thinning medicines for another condition, so the discharge plan matters.

## P08 · Risks and long-term care

Bleeding, infection, rhythm changes and stroke are possible after surgery. Over time the autograft can stretch or leak, and the donor conduit can narrow or leak. Either position may need treatment again. The team arranges lifelong valve imaging, discusses blood pressure and explains what recovery and activity guidance apply to you.

## P09 · One patient valve and one donor valve

Remember the two positions. The patient's pulmonary valve moves to the aorta. The donated valve completes the route to the lungs. Both need ongoing checks. In the next episode, we look at an aortic root operation that keeps the patient's own aortic valve in place.
