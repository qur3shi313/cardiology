# Complete repair of tetralogy of Fallot

Elysian Biomedical LLP · Cardiac Surgery Education Series · Episode 19

English narration for ElevenLabs Alexander. Say Fallot as “fah-LOH.” Record the paragraphs only.

## P01 · Two changes in one repair

Follow this blue marker as it leaves the right ventricle. In this heart, the route to the lungs is tight. Some blood crosses an opening and reaches the aorta instead. Now watch the two changes made during a complete tetralogy of Fallot repair. A patch closes the opening between the ventricles, and a wider passage lets more blood travel from the right ventricle toward the lungs.

## P02 · The four features

Tetralogy of Fallot is present at birth. There is an opening between the two pumping chambers. The aorta sits partly over that opening, so it can receive blood from both ventricles. The way out of the right ventricle toward the lungs is narrowed. The muscular wall of the right ventricle also becomes thicker. These features change how much blood reaches the lungs for oxygen.

## P03 · Why the timing varies

A heart ultrasound shows the opening, where the aorta sits, and how much room blood has to get through to the lungs. The team considers the baby's oxygen levels, symptoms, growth and heart anatomy. Complete repair is often performed in infancy. Some babies need help with lung blood flow sooner, and the team decides when the child is ready for complete repair.

## P04 · The first patch closes the opening

During open-heart surgery, a heart-lung machine supports circulation. Look inside this teaching model at the wall between the right and left ventricles. The surgeon closes the opening with a patch. The patch becomes part of the dividing wall and guides blood from the left ventricle into the aorta, which still sits over the original opening.

## P05 · Opening the route to the lungs

The team also relieves the narrowing. In this example, it removes some thickened muscle beneath the pulmonary valve. The valve ring is too small to leave as it is, so a second patch widens the outflow passage across that ring. This is called a transannular patch. It is part of this example. The exact repair depends on where the narrowing is and whether the valve can be preserved.

## P06 · Follow the repaired routes

Now follow both routes again. Blood from the right ventricle can pass through the widened outflow to the pulmonary arteries and lungs. Blood returning from the lungs enters the left side of the heart and is pumped into the aorta. The two patches do different jobs. One separates the ventricles. The other enlarges the passage to the lungs. The aorta itself has not been moved.

## P07 · What the team checks

After repair, the team watches the child's circulation and heart rhythm. An ultrasound can show whether there is still a small leak around the patch or any remaining narrowing toward the lungs. It can also show whether blood leaks backwards through the pulmonary valve. When a patch crosses the valve ring, the valve can leak because it may no longer close tightly.

## P08 · Follow-up through adulthood

Regular congenital heart follow-up is part of the repair plan, even when a child or adult feels well. Heart ultrasound, an electrocardiogram and sometimes a heart MRI help the team follow valve leakage, right-heart size and rhythm. If a valve leak or narrowing becomes important later, the team may discuss further treatment. Ask what the repair changed and which parts of the heart need regular checks.

## P09 · What to remember

Complete repair closes the opening and improves the route to the lungs. The first patch belongs in the wall. The second patch in this example belongs at the outlet. In the finale we will follow a different problem present at birth, where two great arteries are connected to the wrong pumping chambers.
