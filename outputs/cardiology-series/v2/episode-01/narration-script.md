# Coronary angiogram through the wrist or groin

Cardiology Education Series · Video 1 · Elysian Biomedical LLP

Alexander's existing recordings, in edit order. Exact extract windows are in [the production timeline](timeline.json).

## P01 · What a coronary angiogram shows

A coronary angiogram answers a question: is there a narrowing or blockage in an artery that supplies the heart muscle?

## P02 · Why the test is done

The heart muscle gets its blood supply from the coronary arteries. Plaque can build up in an artery wall and make the channel narrower. An angiogram may be planned after a review of symptoms and other tests. In some suspected heart attacks, the team may need an urgent angiogram and may go on to treat the artery.

## P03 · Preparing for the test

For a planned angiogram, your team explains the test and reviews your medicines, kidney health and any previous reaction to contrast. Follow their instructions about food, drink and medicines.

## P04 · Find the right radial artery

The radial artery runs on the thumb side of the wrist. Here the team checks the right wrist and uses ultrasound to see the artery.

## P05 · Enter the radial artery

Once the area is numb, a small access needle enters the radial artery.

## P06 · Pass the short access wire

A short access wire passes through the hollow needle and into the radial artery. The wire points up the arm.

## P07 · Withdraw the wrist needle

The needle slides back out over the wire. Watch how the wire stays in the artery and preserves the entry path.

## P08 · Place the radial sheath

The short radial sheath goes in over the access wire. A tapered insert, called a dilator, sits inside the sheath as it goes in.

## P09 · Leave the radial sheath in place

The team removes the short access wire and the dilator. The outer sheath stays at the wrist. It gives the next instruments a way into the artery.

## P10 · Pass the longer diagnostic guidewire

A longer diagnostic guidewire passes through the sheath. Live X-ray, called fluoroscopy, lets the cardiologist see where the wire is going.

## P11 · Advance the diagnostic catheter

The diagnostic catheter advances over the long guidewire. The short sheath remains where the team entered the artery.

## P12 · Follow the wrist route to the heart

From the wrist, the wire and catheter follow arteries up the arm, through the shoulder, and into the large artery that leads to the aorta.

## P13 · Find the right common femoral artery

The common femoral artery runs beneath the skin in the upper thigh near the groin.

## P14 · Enter the common femoral artery

After numbing the site, the cardiologist uses a fine access needle to enter the common femoral artery under ultrasound.

## P15 · Pass the fine groin access wire

A fine access wire passes through the hollow needle. X-ray helps the team confirm that the wire follows the main iliac artery.

## P16 · Withdraw the groin needle

The needle slides back out over the wire.

## P17 · Place the temporary introducer

The team passes a temporary micropuncture introducer with its inner dilator over the fine wire.

## P18 · Leave the temporary introducer

The fine wire and inner dilator are taken out. The outer introducer stays in the artery so the team can exchange wires.

## P19 · Pass the exchange wire

A larger exchange wire goes through the temporary introducer and into the artery.

## P20 · Withdraw the temporary introducer

The temporary introducer comes out over the exchange wire. The exchange wire stays in place.

## P21 · Place the working femoral sheath

The working femoral sheath and its dilator slide in over the exchange wire.

## P22 · Leave the working femoral sheath

The team removes the exchange wire and the dilator. The working sheath stays at the groin. The team can take a separate groin image to check the entry site.

## P23 · Follow the groin route to the heart

The wire and catheter travel through the right external and common iliac arteries, then up the abdominal and chest aorta. They round the aortic arch toward the aortic root, where the coronary arteries begin above the aortic valve.

## P24 · Withdraw the long guidewire

At the aortic root, the long guidewire is withdrawn from the catheter. Here the curved catheter tip is positioned at the left coronary opening, above the aortic valve.

## P25 · Record the left coronary arteries

The cardiologist injects X-ray contrast through the diagnostic catheter. For a moment, it outlines the channel that blood travels through. Watch the left main artery fill and then see two of its main branches come into view. Now look at the same moment on the X-ray. The dark branching lines show where the contrast is flowing.

## P26 · Withdraw the first diagnostic catheter

In this groin example, the team uses a different catheter for the right artery. The left catheter moves back from the coronary opening and is withdrawn. The working sheath stays at the groin.

## P27 · Position the right diagnostic catheter

The right diagnostic catheter follows a guidewire up through the aorta. The team withdraws the wire and positions the catheter tip at the right coronary opening.

## P28 · Record the right coronary artery

The cardiologist injects contrast separately at the right coronary opening. The right coronary artery and its branches briefly appear on X-ray. The imaging arm can move around the patient to record the arteries from different angles.

## P29 · What a narrowing looks like

On this example image, the contrast-filled channel is thinner at one spot.

## P30 · What happens next

The next step depends on the findings, the symptoms, and whether the situation is urgent. It may be medicines, another test, angioplasty, or a discussion about bypass surgery. Angioplasty uses a balloon and may place a stent to treat a narrowing. In selected cases the team can do that in the same session.

## P31 · Withdraw the diagnostic catheter

After the images are complete, the cardiologist withdraws the diagnostic catheter through the sheath. The sheath is still at the access site.

## P32 · Remove the groin sheath and apply pressure

For manual closure, the team removes the groin sheath and presses firmly over the entry site to control bleeding.

## P33 · A closure device is another option

In selected cases, the team uses a closure device, such as a plug or stitch system. Staff check the site and leg circulation before the patient gets up.

## P34 · Recovery after groin access

Staff check your pulse, blood pressure, groin and leg circulation. You may need to lie flat with the leg straight while the site is monitored.

## P35 · Remove the wrist sheath and apply a band

Staff position a wrist compression band over the puncture as the sheath comes out. The band puts pressure over the site to control bleeding.

## P36 · Check the wrist and hand

Staff check the puncture site and circulation in the hand. They gradually reduce the band pressure and remove it when it is safe.

## P37 · Who can go home the same day

After an uncomplicated planned test, many patients can go home the same day. Others need longer observation.

## P38 · Care at home

Follow the written instructions for the dressing, activity, medicines and follow-up. A small bruise or mild soreness can happen. Contact the care team promptly for increasing redness or pain, drainage or fever.

## P39 · When to get emergency help

Call local emergency services for heavy bleeding or bleeding that continues despite firm pressure, rapidly growing swelling, a newly cold or numb hand, new chest pressure or sudden shortness of breath.

## P40 · Before you go home

Before you go home, make sure you know your result and what to do during recovery.
