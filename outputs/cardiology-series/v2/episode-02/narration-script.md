# Wrist versus groin access and why the team chooses a route

Cardiology Education Series · Video 2 · Elysian Biomedical LLP

The exact reviewed English narration from Alexander accompanies this timeline.

## P01 · Wrist vs groin access

For a coronary angiogram, the team may go through your wrist or your groin. Both routes reach the arteries that supply your heart. The choice can affect your recovery.

## P02 · Two routes to the heart

Wrist access enters the radial artery on the thumb side. Groin access enters the common femoral artery at the top of the thigh. The team numbs the skin and guides a thin tube called a catheter through the arteries to the heart.

## P03 · An angiogram or a treatment

An angiogram uses contrast and X-rays to show narrowings. Percutaneous coronary intervention, or PCI, is a catheter-based treatment. The team may use a balloon or a stent to treat a narrowing. Either route can be used for the test or treatment. Wrist access is common for a planned angiogram when suitable.

## P04 · Why the wrist is often chosen

For PCI during a heart attack or another acute coronary syndrome, guidelines prefer the wrist when feasible. In that group it lowers the risk of bleeding and blood-vessel complications. The US guideline also reports a lower risk of death. For PCI in stable coronary disease, wrist access helps reduce bleeding and vessel problems at the entry site.

## P05 · When the groin may be chosen

The groin artery can fit larger equipment, including some devices that help the heart pump in emergencies. The team may also use it if the wrist artery is small or hard to pass through. Previous bypass surgery or plans to use a wrist artery for bypass can affect the choice.

## P06 · If the team changes route

Sometimes a wrist artery tightens around the catheter or the path is difficult to navigate. The team may switch to the other wrist or to the groin. If two sites were used, staff check both and explain how to look after them.

## P07 · Closing the access site

At the wrist, staff usually use a pressure band. They gradually ease the pressure while checking the site and your hand. At the groin, they may press by hand or use a closure device, such as a plug or stitch. They check for bleeding with either route.

## P08 · Getting up after the procedure

After wrist access you can often sit up sooner. After groin access you may need to lie flat with your leg straight. When you can walk depends on how the site was closed, the medicines you received and how you’re doing. Wait for staff to tell you it’s safe.

## P09 · Going home

Some people go home the same day after a straightforward planned angiogram. After a heart attack or if you need more monitoring, you may stay longer. Ask for written instructions on your dressing, medicines, lifting, driving and work, and whom to call with concerns.

## P10 · When to contact your care team

A small bruise and mild soreness at either site can happen. Contact your care team promptly if pain, redness or swelling increases, if fluid drains from the site, or if you develop a fever.

## P11 · When to get emergency help

If the site starts bleeding, apply firm pressure. Call your local emergency number for heavy bleeding, bleeding that won’t stop with pressure, or a swelling that grows quickly. Get emergency help if the hand or foot on that side suddenly becomes cold, pale or numb, or if you have severe chest pain or sudden trouble breathing.
