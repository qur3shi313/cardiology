# Recovery and next steps after a coronary angiogram

Elysian Biomedical LLP · Cardiology Education Series · v2 Video 5 · Approved topic 05

English narration for Alexander. Deliver the first question in the first five seconds. Use a measured, calm voice for the urgent-symptom section. End card is silent.

## P01 · What happens after the angiogram?

The angiogram is over. What happens before you go home? Recovery starts with checking the place where the catheter entered, then making sure you understand the result and your follow-up plan.

## P02 · Closing the access site

Once the equipment comes out, staff control bleeding where it entered. At the wrist, they often use a compression band and ease the pressure as they check the site. At the groin, the team may apply pressure or use a closure device. The way they close the site depends on the procedure and the patient.

## P03 · What staff check

In recovery, staff check your blood pressure and pulse, look for bleeding, and assess circulation in the hand or foot on the side used. Depending on the access site and how it was closed, you may be asked to stay lying down at first. Staff tell you when to sit up and walk.

## P04 · Understanding the result

A clinician explains what the pictures showed and what that means for your care. They may discuss medicines and prevention, more tests, angioplasty, or a review for bypass surgery. Sometimes more assessment is needed because the pictures alone do not explain the symptoms. Ask when you will receive any results that are still pending.

## P05 · Before leaving the hospital

Some people can leave after observation following a straightforward planned angiogram. Others need longer monitoring or a hospital stay, especially if they have been treated for a heart attack or had another procedure. Before you leave, ask for written instructions, who to contact, and whether someone should accompany you home.

## P06 · The written recovery plan

Your discharge plan should explain what to do about the dressing and activity, including lifting, driving and work. Ask which medicines to take and what has changed. A stent can change the medicine plan, so check with your care team before stopping or changing medicines prescribed after one.

## P07 · What to watch at the access site

A small bruise or mild soreness can occur. Contact your care team promptly if pain or swelling increases, if the site becomes more red or starts draining, or if you develop a fever. The team can tell you how to get advice and what to do if the clinic is closed.

## P08 · When to get emergency help

Get emergency help for heavy bleeding, bleeding that will not stop, or a rapidly growing swelling at the access site. Sudden coldness, paleness or numbness in the hand or foot on that side also needs urgent attention. Severe chest pain or sudden trouble breathing are emergency symptoms too.

## P09 · What follow-up is for

Follow-up is where you can ask what the findings mean for your symptoms, how the medicines fit into your plan and whether another test or treatment is needed. If the team recommends cardiac rehabilitation after a heart attack or angioplasty, the recovery plan can explain how to join.

## P10 · Before you go

Three things are worth having in writing. What did the angiogram show? What is your plan for recovery and medicines? Who should you contact if you have a problem? The answers may be different for someone who had a test alone and someone who also had treatment.
