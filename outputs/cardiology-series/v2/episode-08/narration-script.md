## P01 · Coronary drug-coated balloons

A heart artery can narrow again inside a stent. In selected cases, a drug-coated balloon can treat that narrowing without leaving a second metal scaffold. Here’s what happens from the first images to the final check and recovery.

## P02 · Why a stented artery can narrow

Coronary arteries carry blood to the heart muscle. When a segment narrows again inside a stent or near its edge, doctors call it in-stent restenosis. Tissue can build up inside the stent. Sometimes the stent itself did not open fully. The cause matters because it helps determine which treatment the team chooses.

## P03 · Imaging and the guidewire

An angiogram uses contrast and X-rays to show the narrowed channel. The team may also use imaging from inside the artery to see how the stent opened and what’s inside it. When treatment goes ahead, a thin coronary guidewire crosses the narrowed segment and provides a track for the balloon catheters.

## P04 · Preparing the narrowed segment

First, the team prepares the narrowed segment. In this example, a separate preparation balloon goes over the wire and expands inside the old stent. The team deflates and removes that balloon, then checks the opening and blood flow. The guidewire stays in place while the team decides whether to proceed with the drug-coated balloon.

## P05 · How the coating delivers medicine

The drug-coated balloon travels over the same wire. The medicine is on the outside of the balloon. At the treatment site, the team inflates it so the coating contacts the inner surface of the treated segment. Some of the medicine transfers into the tissue. Its purpose is to reduce the chance of the artery narrowing again.

## P06 · Balloon out and final check

The team deflates and removes the coated balloon. The original stent stays in place. The team takes more contrast X-rays to see how open the treated area is and how blood flows beyond it. If the result is inadequate, the team may need to use a different treatment.

## P07 · Where drug-coated balloons are used

A drug-coated balloon is one option for selected cases of in-stent restenosis. In twenty twenty-four, the United States Food and Drug Administration approved one coronary device for that use. Researchers have also studied these balloons in some small coronary arteries that had never been stented. Approved uses vary by country and device.

## P08 · Recovery and the medicine plan

After the catheters are removed, the care team controls bleeding at the wrist or groin and monitors recovery. Follow your own discharge instructions, including the plan for antiplatelet medicines. Check with your cardiology team before changing them. Get emergency help for severe or persistent chest pain or bleeding that won’t stop.

## P09 · What stays in the artery

The coated balloon delivers medicine and then comes out. In the example you’ve seen, the original stent stays, and the cardiology team uses the final images to guide what happens next.
